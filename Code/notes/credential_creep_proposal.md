
# Credential Creep, Local Demand, and the Geographic Supply of College Graduates

This project studies **credential creep** within local labor markets. The outcome is the share of workers in occupation \(o\), metropolitan area \(m\), and year \(t\) who hold at least a bachelor's degree,
\[
y_{omt} \equiv \frac{N^{BA}_{omt}}{N_{omt}}.
\]
The motivating question is whether rising educational attainment within occupations reflects changes in the tasks employers need performed, or instead changes in the local availability of degree holders. That distinction matters for interpretation. If the college share of teachers, nurses, managers, technicians, or sales workers rises because local industry demand shifts toward more educated labor, then rising BA shares are evidence of occupational upgrading in the usual sense. If the same occupations become more college-intensive because local labor markets receive an exogenous increase in the supply of college graduates, then rising BA shares are better understood as **credential creep**: employers raise educational requirements because degree holders are more plentiful.

The paper uses LinkedIn data linking workers to colleges, occupations, and labor markets over roughly the last 25 years. Those data are central to the design. Relative to the ACS, LinkedIn includes specific colleges rather than only degree attainment. Relative to state administrative records or LEHD-style linked employer-employee data, it provides national coverage and direct measures of cross-state college pipelines. I use those features to construct two distinct shifters: a Bartik-style measure of **local demand for college labor** and a college-network measure of **local supply of college graduates**. The empirical goal is not simply to ask whether credential creep is driven by supply or demand in isolation. Rather, the goal is to decompose changes in the college share of local occupations into components associated with demand-side upgrading and components associated with the geographic reallocation of educated workers.

## Demand shifter

Let \(i\) index industries. Define \(s_{mi0}\) as MSA \(m\)'s baseline employment share in industry \(i\), measured in an initial year \(0\). Let \(\ell_{it}^{-\!m}\) denote the leave-one-out national BA share in industry \(i\) at time \(t\), excluding MSA \(m\), and let
\[
\Delta \ell_{it}^{-\!m} \equiv \ell_{it}^{-\!m} - \ell_{i,t-1}^{-\!m}.
\]
The demand shifter for MSA \(m\) in year \(t\) is
\[
D_{mt} \equiv \sum_i s_{mi0}\,\Delta \ell_{it}^{-\!m}.
\]
This is the predicted change in the BA share of the local labor force if each industry in MSA \(m\) experienced the same leave-one-out national shift in college intensity as the corresponding industry elsewhere in the country. Intuitively, places specialized in industries that nationally become more college-intensive should face stronger growth in the local demand for college labor, even absent local shocks.

## Supply shifter

The supply design begins from the observation that state higher-ed funding cuts can reallocate students across nearby college markets. A cut in Illinois does not merely reduce educational capacity in Illinois; it can also increase the number of Illinois students attending public institutions in Michigan, Wisconsin, or Indiana. Since college attendance location partly determines early-career labor-market location, such reallocation can increase the later supply of college graduates in the destination labor market.

Let \(c\) index colleges, \(q\) origin states, and let \(g(c)\) denote the state in which college \(c\) is located. Using pre-period LinkedIn data, define
\[
w_{mc0} \equiv \Pr(c \mid m,0),
\]
the baseline share of workers in MSA \(m\) who attended college \(c\). These weights summarize the college-to-MSA pipeline. Next define
\[
r_{qc0} \equiv \Pr(c \mid q,0),
\]
the baseline probability that a student from origin state \(q\) attends college \(c\). Let \(H_{qt}\) denote the higher-ed funding shock in origin state \(q\) at time \(t\), scaled per student or per capita. Predicted enrollment pressure on college \(c\) generated by cuts in other states is
\[
\widetilde{E}_{ct} \equiv \sum_{q \neq g(c)} r_{qc0}\,H_{qt}.
\]
The MSA-level supply shifter is then
\[
S_{mt} \equiv \sum_c w_{mc0}\,\widetilde{E}_{ct}.
\]
An MSA therefore receives a larger predicted supply shock when it is historically connected to colleges that disproportionately absorb students displaced by funding cuts in other states. The identifying variation comes from external fiscal shocks and pre-existing college pipelines rather than contemporaneous expansion by local colleges.

This construction identifies a shock to the **geographic allocation** of college graduates, not necessarily to aggregate national graduate production. That distinction is appropriate for a paper on local credential creep. The relevant margin is whether local employers respond differently when the nearby supply of degree holders increases for reasons that originate outside the local labor market.

## Empirical strategy

The empirical design treats credential creep as a local labor-market outcome and asks whether it is better explained by shocks to the **demand** for college labor or shocks to the **local supply** of college graduates. The baseline outcome is the college share within occupation \(o\), metropolitan area \(m\), and year \(t\):
\[
y_{omt} \equiv \frac{\#\{\text{workers with BA in }(o,m,t)\}}{\#\{\text{workers in }(o,m,t)\}}.
\]
The core empirical challenge is that both shifters of interest vary primarily at the MSA-year level, while the outcome varies at the occupation-MSA-year level. The design therefore combines MSA-year shocks with predetermined occupation-level heterogeneity. This framing is also helpful conceptually: the paper is not simply asking whether some places become more college-intensive, but whether local supply and demand shocks induce **differential educational upgrading across occupations** within the same local labor market. 

A specification with rich MSA-year controls cannot identify the effects of \(D_{mt}\) and \(S_{mt}\) from cross-MSA-year variation alone, because both shifters vary at the MSA-year level. For that reason, the preferred specification interacts each shifter with predetermined occupation-level characteristics. In the earlier draft this role was played by a generic \(\kappa_o\). In the revised version, I would avoid a single generic term and instead use two explicit occupation-level modifiers, because they capture different mechanisms. The first captures whether an occupation has **room for further educational upgrading**; the second captures whether an occupation is especially exposed to **local nontradable or population-serving demand**. This makes the regression easier to interpret and keeps the empirical strategy aligned with the substantive question.

### Occupation-level modifier 1: room for credential creep

The first occupation characteristic is an index of how much scope an occupation has for further increases in BA share. Let \(\bar y_{o0}\) denote the baseline national BA share in occupation \(o\). Then one natural measure is
\[
\kappa^{\text{slack}}_o \equiv 1-\bar y_{o0},
\]
which is large for occupations far from full BA saturation. A more flexible alternative is
\[
\kappa^{\text{interior}}_o \equiv \bar y_{o0}(1-\bar y_{o0}),
\]
which peaks for occupations in the interior of the education distribution and is smaller for occupations already near 0 or 1. The first measure corresponds directly to the idea that some occupations are already near an educational ceiling. The second corresponds to the idea that the occupations most susceptible to credential creep are those with meaningful room to move in either direction, rather than occupations that are already almost entirely non-BA or almost entirely BA. 

### Occupation-level modifier 2: local-service exposure

The second occupation characteristic is an index of how strongly an occupation is tied to local, population-serving demand. Let \(\kappa^{\text{local}}_o\) denote a predetermined continuous measure of local-service exposure, constructed from historical data as the responsiveness of occupation-level employment to local population growth, or from an analogous nontradability index. This characteristic is distinct from educational slack. An occupation can have substantial room for upgrading but not be particularly local, or vice versa. Using both modifiers allows the estimates to distinguish whether supply and demand shocks matter most in occupations with room for educational upgrading, in occupations tied to local consumption, or in occupations satisfying both conditions. 

### Preferred specification

The preferred estimating equation is
\[
y_{omt}
=
\beta_D^{\text{slack}}\big(D_{mt}\times \kappa_o^{\text{slack}}\big)
+
\beta_S^{\text{slack}}\big(S_{mt}\times \kappa_o^{\text{slack}}\big)
+
\beta_D^{\text{local}}\big(D_{mt}\times \kappa_o^{\text{local}}\big)
+
\beta_S^{\text{local}}\big(S_{mt}\times \kappa_o^{\text{local}}\big)
+
\alpha_{om}
+
\gamma_{ot}
+
\delta_{mt}
+
\varepsilon_{omt}.
\]
Here, \(\alpha_{om}\) are occupation-by-MSA fixed effects, \(\gamma_{ot}\) are occupation-by-year fixed effects, and \(\delta_{mt}\) are MSA-by-year fixed effects. The occupation-by-MSA fixed effects absorb time-invariant differences in local occupational composition; the occupation-by-year fixed effects absorb national trends in educational upgrading within occupations; and the MSA-by-year fixed effects absorb any local labor-market shock common to all occupations within an MSA-year. Identification therefore comes from the fact that the same local supply or demand shock should matter more for occupations with greater room for educational upgrading or greater exposure to local-service demand.

### Interpretation

This specification admits several coherent patterns. If \(\beta_D^{\text{slack}}>0\), then local demand shocks raise BA shares most in occupations with room to upgrade, consistent with genuine occupational upskilling. If \(\beta_S^{\text{slack}}>0\), then local supply shocks raise BA shares most in those same occupations, consistent with credential creep driven by the local abundance of college graduates. If both coefficients are positive, the paper does not collapse into ambiguity. Instead, it implies that observed credential creep reflects two distinct adjustment margins: local occupations become more college-intensive both because the structure of production raises the demand for college labor and because local graduate supply makes it easier for employers to ratchet up educational screens. In that sense, the paper decomposes credential creep into a **demand-driven component** and a **supply-driven component**, rather than treating one as the sole explanation. 

The identification argument should be explicit about the two ways shift-share designs are justified in the recent econometric literature. Goldsmith-Pinkham, Sorkin, and Swift show that Bartik designs can be understood as pooled exposure designs in which identification relies on the exogeneity of the **shares**; viewed this way, a Bartik instrument is numerically equivalent to a weighted combination of the underlying shares. Borusyak, Hull, and Jaravel instead emphasize an alternative quasi-experimental framework in which identification comes from the quasi-random assignment of the **shocks**, while the exposure shares may be endogenous. Adão, Kolesár, and Morales show that shift-share settings also require special attention to inference because residual correlation can arise across observational units with similar shares. In the present application, the demand shifter is most naturally defended as a BHJ-style shock-driven design: national leave-one-out industrial upgrading is the source of identifying variation, while local baseline industry shares encode differential exposure. The supply shifter is similar in spirit: origin-state funding cuts are the shocks, and baseline student-college and college-to-MSA pipelines are the exposure shares. In both cases, the empirical burden is therefore to argue that the shock series are as-good-as-random conditional on controls and fixed effects, and then to use inference procedures appropriate for shift-share settings. 

## Interpreting the coefficients

The paper does not require one coefficient to be zero. If \(\beta_D > 0\) and \(\beta_S > 0\), that implies a coherent decomposition of credential creep into **demand-driven** and **supply-driven** components. In that case, local occupations become more college-intensive for two reasons. First, employers in some places genuinely need more educated labor because local industry composition shifts toward sectors that are becoming more college-intensive nationally. Second, employers in some places raise the educational composition of their workforce because local colleges and migration networks deliver a larger pool of degree holders. Under this interpretation, credential creep is not a residual category after ruling out labor demand; it is a margin of adjustment that can coexist with real occupational upgrading.

That decomposition is economically meaningful. A positive demand effect is consistent with a change in the tasks firms need performed or in the productivity of college labor. A positive supply effect is consistent with employers tightening educational screens when college graduates become easier to hire locally. The relative magnitudes of \(\beta_D\) and \(\beta_S\) then describe how much of observed credential creep reflects changes in the structure of local production versus changes in the local abundance of degree holders. Put differently, the empirical strategy treats rising BA shares within occupations as the outcome of both **who local employers want to hire** and **who is locally available to hire**.

## Identification and objections

The main concern with the demand shifter is the standard one in shift-share designs: baseline industry composition may proxy for persistent local trends rather than only exogenous exposure to national industrial change. Using leave-one-out national growth avoids mechanical bias, but the design still relies on the assumption that differential exposure to national industrial shifts is orthogonal to unobserved local shocks after fixed effects and controls.

The supply shifter raises a different set of concerns. First, the effect of funding cuts depends on the size and composition of the students available for reallocation, so the instrument is best understood as a fiscal shock interacting with pre-period student pipelines rather than as a pure policy shock. Second, the design assumes nearby public universities are not fully capacity constrained. That assumption is testable: colleges more exposed to origin-state cuts should mechanically increase out-of-state enrollment if the first stage is operative. Third, LinkedIn coverage is selective. That concern is important, but it is also why LinkedIn is valuable here. Few other datasets link specific colleges to later occupations and labor-market locations at national scale. I will therefore treat LinkedIn as central to the empirical strategy and reweight the data to align more closely with the observable composition of the U.S. workforce.

The contribution of the paper is to use those unusually rich data to distinguish between local occupational upgrading driven by labor demand and local credential creep driven by the geographic supply of college graduates.


## Brief literature review and positioning

The closest antecedents come from four adjacent literatures rather than a single canonical economics literature on "credential creep." The classic supply-and-demand literature on wage inequality treats changes in the college premium as the outcome of shifts in relative demand and relative supply. Katz and Murphy (1992) formalize that framework for changes in U.S. wage structure, Goldin and Katz (2008) place it in a longer-run historical "race between education and technology," and Card and Lemieux (2001) show that cohort-specific slowdowns in educational attainment can help explain rising returns to college for younger workers.[^katz-murphy] [^goldin-katz] [^card-lemieux] That literature is the natural benchmark for interpreting this project's demand and supply shifters, but it is mostly about wage premia and aggregate relative quantities rather than rising BA shares within occupation-by-place cells.

A second relevant literature studies rising educational requirements in vacancies and employer screening. Modestino, Shoag, and Ballance show that posted education and experience requirements rose during slack labor markets and partially reversed during recovery, which they interpret as evidence that employers use formal requirements strategically when skilled workers are plentiful.[^modestino-shoag-ballance] Hershbein and Kahn show that vacancy skill requirements rose more in MSAs hit harder by the Great Recession and argue that the pattern is consistent with recession-induced restructuring toward more skilled workers and routine-labor saving technologies.[^hershbein-kahn] This project is close to that literature in spirit, but the outcome here is different: I study the realized college share of incumbent workers within occupation × MSA × year cells rather than employer requirements in postings.

A third nearby literature studies local human capital and spatial equilibrium. Moretti documents local spillovers from college attainment and shows that differences in cities' human-capital stocks affect local wage structure.[^moretti] For present purposes, the key implication is conceptual: local outcomes can move through both wages and quantities, so there is no presumption that rising occupational BA shares must reflect either a pure demand story or a pure supply story. A setting with both \(\beta_D > 0\) and \(\beta_S > 0\) is therefore economically coherent. In that case, observed credential creep can be decomposed into a demand-driven component, reflecting genuine occupational upgrading, and a supply-driven component, reflecting tighter educational screening when degree holders become more abundant locally.

The fourth literature, and the one most directly relevant for the supply design, studies college geography, migration, and higher-ed finance. Groen shows that attending college in a state increases the probability of later working in that state, and Winters finds a positive relationship between in-state college enrollment and later-life residence in the birth state.[^groen] [^winters] Kennan develops a model in which geographic variation in college financing affects both college choice and migration decisions of graduates.[^kennan] Conzelmann et al. use LinkedIn to measure college-specific labor markets and show that about half of recent graduates live and work near the institution they attended, with an even larger share remaining in-state.[^conzelmann] Together, those papers provide the core economic logic for the supply shifter proposed here: state higher-ed funding shocks can reallocate students across colleges, and college location helps determine where graduates begin their careers.

I am not aware of a paper that combines all of these elements in a single design: a local credential-creep outcome measured as the BA share within occupation × MSA × year, a Bartik-style demand shifter based on industry composition, and a college-network supply shifter that routes state higher-ed funding cuts through cross-state college pipelines. The existing literature provides the pieces, but not the joint design. That is the gap this project aims to fill.[^modestino-shoag-ballance] [^hershbein-kahn] [^conzelmann] [^kennan]
